In-play model validation · 24 Aug 2026

Does a fixed (p, q) track a live tennis market?

First live test of the two-parameter model against Kalshi, captured game-by-game through a complete match. Two fitting regimes compared: one frozen after two prices, one refitted after every game.

Match Poljicak vs Schoenhaus · ATP Challenger Augsburg · R32 · best of 3
Result Schoenhaus won 6–4, 6–3 · tracked player: Poljicak (underdog)
Data 17 boundary prices, 15 out-of-sample · 1,750 GETs · 4.5 s median cadence

Verdict

Both regimes tracked the market to under three cents RMS across two sets — comparable to the 2.7 c of the earlier hand-logged match. Neither is meaningfully better than the other, and which one wins depends entirely on where you anchor the frozen fit.

The anchor decides the winner

Freezing the static fit on the first two prices makes it win. Freezing it on three, four or five makes it lose — consistently. The single configuration where static leads is also the one built on the two prices that were read by hand off the board rather than captured.

Replication scorecard

Three claims carried over from the hand-logged Sherif–Oliynykova match. One held, one failed outright, one is new.

Failed

Move amplification

median |market move| / |model move| = 0.97 over 16 moves · prior match: 1.47

The strongest claim from the earlier match did not reproduce. Only 8 of 16 moves overshot the model. The mean ratio is 1.61, but that is carried by a single 5.6× outlier — quartiles run 0.13 / 0.75 / 0.89 / 1.36 / 5.62. This market did not systematically over-react, which removes the mechanism that would make "fade the post-game move" tradeable.

Held

Overshoots revert

regression of Δerror on error = −0.65 over 15 points · prior match: −0.77

Roughly two thirds of each deviation came back by the next game boundary. Close to the prior estimate, and the one finding that survived contact with a second match.

New

Underdog bias flips at the set boundary

set 1 running mean +1.48 c · set 2 running mean −1.06 c

Errors ran positive through the back half of set 1, then went −2.9 c the instant the set completed and stayed negative. The prior match showed the same sign flip at the same moment — the market treats a lost set as more damaging than a constant-probability model does. Two matches, same direction.

What the running fit actually did

It overcorrected once, then spent the match walking it back. The −5.0 c miss at 1–3 pushed p from 0.656 to 0.777 — a 97% hold rate and a 2% break rate, an implausible tennis player. That drove its predictions down to 0.25 just as the market was marking Poljicak up, producing the +3.9 and +5.3 misses that followed.

From there p slid monotonically — 0.777, 0.774, 0.753, 0.725, 0.701, 0.693, 0.677, 0.665 — and the errors closed. The static fit never moved and the market came back to it: −5.0, −5.5, +0.5, +1.8, crossing through zero on its own.

The final refitted values (p 0.665, q 0.327) land close to where the static fit started (0.656 / 0.338). Twelve games of adaptation returned to roughly the opening estimate, which is the cleanest single argument that the extra machinery bought nothing here.

Read these numbers with the caveats attached

  • One match, one player, 15 out-of-sample points. Nothing here is significant in any statistical sense. The static-versus-running comparison flips on a configuration choice, which is what an underpowered test looks like.
  • The first two prices were read by hand off the board, not captured. The capture attached at 1–1, so the pre-match anchor and game 1 were supplied manually. The static line that wins at anchor-2 is built entirely on those two.
  • Boundaries for games 2 and 3 are missing. Game 2 completed before the capture attached; game 3 was lost to a poller restart. The 1–3 row is therefore a two-game jump, and it is also the row carrying the worst miss for both regimes.
  • Low-price accuracy is partly mechanical. The last rows (0.065, 0.025) show sub-cent errors, but near the tails there is less room in absolute cents for model and market to disagree. Those rows flatter both RMS figures.
  • Six gaps over 15 s interrupted the tape, two caused by a bug where a Kalshi read-timeout was misread as "match not live" and triggered a 60 s idle. Fixed after the fact; it may have delayed a boundary price.
Method. Game state and both player midpoints polled every 4 s from Kalshi's public milestone and live-data endpoints. A price is recorded the first poll after the game score changes; the market was observed leading the score feed by ~5 s, so captured prices are settled post-game quotes rather than stale ones. Vig stripped by normalising across both player markets. Point score excluded from fitting — it is 0–0 at a game end.
